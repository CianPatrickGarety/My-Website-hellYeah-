Macbeth Conflict Essay - SQA N5 English 2013 Q1

Ambition is a powerful desire for something which can cause people to act in an out-of-character way. In ‘Macbeth,’ a tragedy by William Shakespeare, we see ambition corrupt a high-ranking Scottish thane. Macbeth hears a prophecy from 3 witches that proclaim he will become king of Scotland. This causes Macbeths and Lady Macbeth’s ambitions to grow into uncontrollable monsters before their demise in the climax of the play. Their ambitions are fuelled by the conflict between the two.

The conflict between Lady Macbeth and Macbeth is introduced in the Exposition of the play, where we see a disconnect in their reactions to the witch's prophecy. When Macbeth first hears the prophecy, he writes to Lady Macbeth about the prophecy of becoming King, risking treason against Duncan. He does so because he views Lady Macbeth as an equal – shown by him referring to her as his ‘dearest partner of greatness’ - something unusual in the Jacobean period when the play was written. Lady Macbeth, after hearing the prophecy, declares in a soliloquy


“Come you spirits that tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here”


She immediately wishes for the power to act and commit the masculine act of murder. In this period, women didn’t have much power and needed to rely on men. This is why Lady Macbeth needs to manipulate Macbeth into killing Duncan as opposed to doing it herself. Lady Macbeth calling upon supernatural spirits to do something unnatural ties to the witches Macbeth heard the prophecy from. When Lady Macbeth first hears the prophecy, she jumps at the chance for power, willingly putting herself and Macbeth into conflict to act on it, yet Macbeth doesn’t and is more restrained, he has his ambition in check. After weighing the pros and cons of killing Duncan – a good king who has been nothing but good to Macbeth – Macbeth decides:


“I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition”


Macbeth states his hamartia to the audience, that he has excess ambition which will be his downfall. He compares himself to a horse going too far and dying, showing awareness of what will happen to him if he acts on his ambition. He will be ruined completely and die. Macbeth is on the right side of his inner conflict deciding not to kill Duncan, as he can still be considered a good man at the this point in the play. This furthers the disconnect between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth’s ambitions. Therefore, the conflict between the key characters of Macbeth and Lady Macbeth is introduced in the exposition of the play.

The conflict between the key characters of Lady Macbeth and Macbeth is furthered in the Rising Action of the play through Lady Macbeth’s manipulation. Lady Macbeth and Macbeth get into an argument about the prophecy where Lady Macbeth states:


“When durst you do it, then you were a man”


Lady Macbeth is attacking Macbeth’s Masculine pride to manipulate him into acting on her ambition. In the Jacobean period, Masculinity was equal to strength, so Lady Macbeth saying Macbeth isn’t a man because he won’t act is the same as calling him weak. Macbeth feels incredibly upset at his “dearest partner of greatness” viewing him weak, which pushes him across the border of good and bad into proving himself strong to Lady Macbeth – who knew this violation of trust would work – and killing Duncan. Macbeth resolves to kill Duncan, and just before so says:


“The bell invites me. Hear it not, Duncan, for it is a knell which summons thee to Heaven or to Hell”


This marks the point of the play where Macbeth can no longer be considered a good man. He knows committing regicide will doom him and goes against God yet decides to act on Lady Macbeth’s ambition and kill Duncan anyway. This also marks where Macbeth turns away from God, shown through him not being able to respond Amen to the guards after killing Duncan and turning to the Roman God Neptune to wash away his guilt (Will all great Neptune’s oceans clean me of this deed?). Therefore, the conflict between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth is furthered in the Rising Action through their actions in relation to the witch’s prophecy.

The conflict between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth results in their relationship cracking in the Turning Point of the play. After Macbeth sees the ghost of Banquo – who he ordered killed due to his knowledge of the prophecy and therefore ability to harm him – and freaks out, Lady Macbeth had to save him and usher away all their guests. When she asks what that was all about, Macbeth responds coldly:


“Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck”


The title of dearest chuck is a big downgrade of Lady Macbeth’s previous title of “My dearest partner of greatness", showing Macbeth has lost respect for her and resents her for pushing him into violating the Divine right of Kings and committing regicide for a ‘Barren sceptre and fruitless crown.’ He also talks a lot more secretive and disrespectfully to Lady Macbeth, compared to earlier in the play where he was kind and warm. Later, Macbeth decides to lock in on being King, as it is all he has left, and declares:


“I am in blood stepp’d so deep that, should I wade no more, returning were as tedious as go o’er”


This marks the point in the play where Macbeth turns into a tyrant in a desperate attempt to make his actions have some meaning. This is because of Macbeths fear of being forgotten. He has no children, so when he dies, the last trace of his existence dies with him. He has lost everything, including the close confidant of Lady Macbeth and is clinging onto the last thing he has last, his barren sceptre and fruitless crown. Therefore, the conflict between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth breaks apart their once close relationship in the turning point of the play

The conflict between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth goes unresolved due to both their deaths in the Climax of the play. Lady Macbeth is unable to cope with pushing Macbeth into becoming a tyrant and falls into a state of mental regression:


“To bed, to bed .... come, come, come, come”


Lady Macbeth is unable to cope with the regicide of Duncan and all the innocent lives – such as the Macduff family – killed by Macbeth’s tyranny, as she feels responsible for pushing him into becoming the monster he is currently. She uses asyndetic language to show she has a limited vocabulary due to losing her power and control. She lost her power for messing with the natural order by disrupting the Divine Right of Kings. The doctor looking after her furthers this by saying “Unnatural deeds do breed unnatural troubles.” In contrast, Macbeth has become apathetic. When Macbeth hears of Lady Macbeths death and the surrounding forces ready to kill him, he says in a soliloquy:


“Life ... is a tale told by an idiot, full of fury and signifying nothing”


Macbeth’s pessimistic view of life tells us he has given up. He has accepted that his actions were meaningless, and his life did not matter. He has been made numb by his actions and recognises that he made the wrong choice; if he did not kill Duncan, he would be happier. He has been completely ruined by going against nature and giving into his excess ambition. Shakespeare choose to show this because, in 1605, King James 1 was almost assassinated in the Gunpowder plot, and wanted to squander anymore attempts against the crown. Therefore, the conflict between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth remains unresolved due to their deaths, caused by the consequences of their excess ambitions.

In conclusion, ‘Macbeth’ by William Shakespeare shows how the conflict between two characters occurs and affects the characters of Macbeth and Lady Macbeth, and the events of the play. The conflict arises from their clashing ambitions and causes the downfall and complete ruin of two nobles.

Macbeth Character Essay - SQA N5 English 2013 Model Q2

Ambition is a powerful force which can cause people to act in an out-of-character way. In ‘Macbeth’, a tragedy by William Shakespeare, we see ambition as a fatal flaw in the character of Macbeth. ‘Macbeth’ follows a high-ranking Scottish thane who hears a prophecy from three witches that he will become king of Scotland. This causes a spiral in his ambition, ruining him and ending with his demise in the climax of the play.

We are introduced to Macbeth and his hamartia of excess ambition in the exposition of the play. Macbeth says in a soliloquy – his inner thoughts – about his thoughts on the prophecy;


“Stars hide your fires; let not light see my black and deep desires”


Macbeth is commanding the natural world and God to not see his evil intent, which is very important, as in the Jacobean period, religion was very prominent. Macbeth trying to hide from God shows he is aware that acting on his excess ambition, committing regicide and violating the ‘divine right of kings’ is not a good thing to do. After a monologue where he weighs the pros and cons of killing Duncan – a good ruler who has been nothing but kind – for his own power, Macbeth decides:


“I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition”


Macbeth concludes his only reason for killing Duncan is his own ambition. He compares himself to a horse jumping too far and dying because of it, which suggest he knows the consequences of going against God, making the downfall more tragic. He is on the right side of his inner conflict and can still be considered a good man at this point, as he is still in control of his excess ambition. Therefore, Macbeth’s fatal flaw of excess ambition is introduced in the exposition.

We see Macbeth’s fatal flaw be exploited by his ‘dearest partner of greatness’ - Lady Macbeth – in the rising action of the play. Macbeth sent word to Lady Macbeth about the prophecy – highlighting his trust in her, as he risked treason and death sending it to her – who pushes him to act and become king. Although he initially refuses, Lady Macbeth says:


“When durst you do it, then you were a man”


In the Jacobean period, masculinity was equivalent to strength and honour. By emasculating him and telling him is less of a man, Lady Macbeth has played upon Macbeth’s biggest insecurity, abusing his trust. This fuels Macbeth with a deep need to impress his closest confidant, going against his valiant self and deciding to kill the king. Just before killing Duncan, Macbeth says in a soliloquy:


“The bell invites me. Hear it not, Duncan, for it is a knell which summons thee to heaven or to hell”


Macbeth is speaking very calmly and almost poetically, showing how effective Lady Macbeth’s manipulation was on him. Macbeth again references religious iconography, Heaven and Hell, which was important to the time period, but also to how killing Duncan affected him. Just afterwards, he was unable to say “Amen” to the guards and turns to Neptune (A roman God) to wash away his guilt of Killing Duncan (“Will all great Neptune’s oceans clean me of this deed?”). This marks where Macbeth can no longer be considered a good man, and also where his close relationship with Lady Macbeth starts to deteriorate. Therefore, the fatal flaw of excess ambition within Macbeth is exploited in the Rising Action of the Play.

The fallout of acting on his hamartia is shown in the Turning Point. Macbeth sees the ghost of Banquo – who he ordered killed due to him knowing the prophecy and paranoia - at a dinner party, Lady Macbeth had to usher away all the guests. When she asks what happened, Macbeth coldly responds:


“Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck”


This is a big downgrade from “dearest partner of greatness”, showing Macbeth no longer views her in a positive light. He resents her for pushing him into a “barren sceptre and fruitless crown”. Macbeth’s weakness of excess ambition has caused his once close relationship to blister and crack. His talking quite rude and secretive is a stark contrast with earlier in the play, where he risked treason because he trusted her so much. Macbeth decided that, if he has ruined himself for only Banquo’s children’s gain, he will lock in on holding onto the only thing he has left:


“I am in blood stepp’d so deep, that should I wade no more, returning were as tedious as go o’er”


This marks Macbeth’s turn to a tyrant. He has been consumed by paranoia and fear of losing the crown, like Duncan did. He has lost the close confidant of his wife, who made the plans, and begun acting rash. He proceeds to kill the innocent Macduff family and many more on his whims in a desperate attempt to make his action have meaning. This is because of his fear of being forgotten, as he has no children, when he dies so too will the last trace of him die also. Shakespeare showed Macbeth’s complete ruin to appease King James I, as in 1605, King James was nearly assassinated in the gunpowder plot of 1605, and Shakespeare wanted to convey the complete ruination awaiting those that oppose the crown – and through the divine right of Kings – God. Therefore, the Turning Point shows the harmful effects of Macbeth acting on his weakness of excess ambition.

The eventual fate of Macbeth is shown in the climax of the play. He hears that Lady Macbeth is dead, and enemy forces are surrounding the castle and proclaims:


“Life... is tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing”


Macbeth’s nihilist view shows his ambition has completely burned out. The once noble and honourable thane thought highly of by everyone, now detested and alone. He realises his life had no meaning and nothing mattered. He also realised he would have been happier if he didn’t kill Duncan, highlighting his tragedy. We are also shown again his fear of being forgotten, as he goes on to say” Then is heard no more”. Shakespeare intentionally shows Macbeth’s complete ruin due to his weakness and hamartia of excess ambition, as a warning to people in the Jacobean period about going against the king. Therefore, the fate of those that oppose the crown is shown in the climax of the play.

In conclusion, ‘Macbeth’ by William Shakespeare effectively shows how Macbeth’s weakness of excess ambition causes the death of the king, destruction of his relationship with Lady Macbeth and his complete ruin. He goes from a highly respected thane and noble warrior to a paranoid, lonely man clinging to the last thing he has, being called a ‘butcher’ after his death.
